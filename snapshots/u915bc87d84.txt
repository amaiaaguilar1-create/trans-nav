Parties in Doe v. Ladapo, a case challenging Florida&#8217;s ban on healthcare for transgender youth and restrictions on the medical interventions available to trans adults, presented oral arguments on Wednesday before the U.S. Court of Appeals for the 11th Circuit in Atlanta.
The case was appealed by defendants representing the Sunshine State following a decision in June 2024 by Judge Robert Hinkle of the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Florida, who found &#8220;the law and rules unconstitutional and unenforceable on equal protection grounds,&#8221; according to a press release from the National Center for Lesbian Rights, which is involved in the litigation on behalf of the plaintiffs.
The district court additionally found the Florida healthcare ban unconstitutional on the grounds that it was &#8220;motivated by purposeful discrimination against transgender people,&#8221; though the ban and restrictions will remain in effect pending a decision by the appellate court.
Joining NCLR in the lawsuit are attorneys from GLAD Law, the Human Rights Campaign, Southern Legal Counsel, and the law firms Lowenstein Sandler and Jenner and Block.
&#8220;As a mother who simply wants to protect and love my child for who she is, I pray that the Eleventh Circuit will affirm the district court’s thoughtful and powerful order, restoring access to critical healthcare for all transgender Floridians,&#8221; plaintiff Jane Doe said. &#8220;No one should have to go through what my family has experienced.”
&#8220;As a transgender adult just trying to live my life and care for my family, it is so demeaning that the state of Florida thinks it’s their place to dictate my healthcare decisions,&#8221; said plaintiff Lucien Hamel.
&#8220;Members of the legislature have referred to the high quality healthcare I have received, which has allowed me to live authentically as myself, as ‘mutilation’ and ‘an abomination’ and have called the providers of this care ‘evil,’&#8221; Hamel added. &#8220;We hope the appellate court sees these rules and laws for what truly are: cruel.”
“Transgender adults don’t need state officials looking over their shoulders, and families of transgender youth don’t need the government dictating how to raise their children,” said Shannon Minter, legal director of NCLR. “The district court heard the evidence and found that these restrictions are based on bias, not science. The court of appeals should affirm that judgment.”
Noting Hinkle&#8217;s conclusion that the ban and restrictions were &#8220;motivated by animus, not science or evidence,&#8221; Simone Chris, who leads Southern Legal Counsel&#8217;s Transgender Rights Initiative, said, “The state has loudly and proudly enacted bans on transgender people accessing healthcare, using bathrooms, transgender teachers using their pronouns and titles, and a slough of other actions making it nearly impossible for transgender individuals to live in this state.&#8221;
Lowenstein Sandler Partner Thomas Redburn said, “ The defendants have offered nothing on appeal that could serve as a valid basis for overturning that finding&#8221; by the district court.
“Not only does this dangerous law take away parents’ freedom to make responsible medical decisions for their child, it inserts the government into private health care matters that should be between adults and their providers,&#8221; said Jennifer Levi, senior director of transgender and queer rights at GLAD Law.

Ruby Corado files motion for early release from prison
Former Casa Ruby director pleaded guilty to wire fraud

By
Lou Chibbaro Jr.
Ruby Corado in El Salvador in 2022. (Washington Blade photo by Ernesto Valle)
Ruby Corado, the founder and former executive director of the now closed LGBTQ community services organization Casa Ruby, has reported being raped and sexually abused or harassed at least 31 times since being sentenced to federal prison on Jan. 13, 2026, according to a motion seeking early release filed by her attorney.
The motion, filed Nov. 13 in U.S. District Court for D.C. by a federal public defender attorney, asks a federal judge to grant Corado an early release at this time rather than complete her sentence of 33 months of incarceration after pleading guilty to a charge of wire fraud.
The motion says concerns raised by Corado’s attorney at the time of her sentencing hearing in January that, as a transgender woman, she would be subjected to sexual abuse, has happened in a big way.
The motion notes that under the current federal prison policy set by the Trump-Vance administration’s Department of Justice, trans women are assigned to male prisons and male prison housing facilities. It points out that since her initial incarceration in January Corado has spent time in federal prisons in Oklahoma, Louisiana, and Texas.
“Ms. Corado signed a declaration under penalty of perjury detailing her experiences in Bureau of Prisons custody, including the 31 rapes and sexual assaults to which she has been subjected,” the motion states. “She has, for instance, woken up to men masturbating in her face; male officers have stripped her naked in front of 400 other inmates during processing; she has been forced to wear male clothing 32 years after transitioning; other inmates regularly jump into her shower aroused and expecting sex; and her cellmates slap her butt when she is trying to sleep,” her attorney’s court motion states.
The motion, filed by federal public defender attorney Joanna Muson Perales, notes that existing federal law and prison regulations allow for a court to grant a reduced sentence and early release from a prison if “extraordinary and compelling reasons warrant” an early release.
“Altogether with Ms. Corado’s deteriorating mental health, other health conditions, and rehabilitation, these bases are extraordinary and compelling reasons to reduce her sentence to time served,” the motion adds.
Online court records show that U.S. District Court Judge Trevor M. McFadden, who continues to preside over the case, has yet to issue a decision on the motion and has not said how long he may take to deliberate over the motion.
As of Nov. 16, the online court records show that prosecutors from the Office of the U.S. Attorney for D.C. had not yet responded to the motion. Perales couldn’t immediately be reached for comment.
Among other things, the motion includes a 16-page handwritten declaration by Corado describing in detail the instances of rape, sexual harassment, and alleged mistreatment she says she has experienced since being incarcerated in January. It says prison officials for the most part failed to take action against those she accused of sexually assaulting her, claiming, according to Corado, that her allegations were “unsubstantiated” after her attackers denied the allegations.
In January, McFadden sentenced Corado to 33 months of incarceration. He also sentenced her to 24 months of supervised release upon her completion of incarceration.
In addition to the incarceration, McFadden agreed to a request by federal prosecutors to hold Corado responsible for “restitution” and “forfeiture” in the amount of $956,215 that prosecutors said she illegally misappropriated from federal COVID-19 related federal loans obtained by Casa Ruby.
At the time she pleaded guilty to the wire fraud charge Corado admitted to allegations by prosecutors that she illegally transferred at least $180,000 of the COVID relief funds obtained by Casa Ruby to private bank accounts in El Salvador, where she is from. But Corado claimed her intent was to use the funds to start a Casa Ruby facility in El Salvador and not to use the funds for her personal benefit.

The family of a lesbian who a U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement officer killed in Minneapolis in January has filed two federal lawsuits.
The Associated Press notes Renee Good’s partner, Becca Good, and her brother, Brent Ganger, in the lawsuits accuse the Trump-Vance administration and ICE officer Jonathan Ross of “unjustified and excessive use of force.”
Renee Good was a 37-year-old mother of three.
Ross on Jan. 7 shot and killed her in Minneapolis.
The shooting took place during the Trump-Vance administration’s so-called Operation Metro Surge that targeted Somalis and other immigrant groups in Minneapolis. U.S. Customs and Border Protection agents on Jan. 24 shot and killed Alex Pretti, a 37-year-old nurse who worked for the U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs, in Minneapolis.
Both shooting sparked outrage across the U.S. and around the world.
“Every morning, she is my first thought. I think about how the kids are growing up without their mama, and how I will grow old without the love of my life. I think about how we should have had 40 more years together. But I also think about what she would have wanted after all of this,” said Becca Good in a statement the Human Rights Campaign released after she and her partner&#8217;s family announced the lawsuits.
“As we have tried to navigate our own massive loss, I am also devastated by how many other lives have been destroyed in this darkest of times. So many other families are dealing with the loss of their loved ones; to everyone who has had to witness it — the pain of this moment extends far beyond our family,” added Becca Good. “What happened to us should never happen to any family. No child should grow up without a parent because of it. No one should lose their sister or their daughter. No one should have to live without their soulmate. Accountability — real accountability — is the only way to make sure that no other family has to go through what we have.”
HRC President Kelley Robinson in her own statement said the Good family “is one of our own — and today, the Human Rights Campaign joins them in demanding justice.”
“It has been eight months and 24 days since Renee was shot and killed by an ICE agent, and the United States government has failed to take responsibility for its actions,” said Robinson. “Her partner, Becca, and brother Brent, have made the brave and bold decision to hold the government accountable, and we stand with them. Like every person murdered by ICE, Renee should be alive today. The Goods were robbed of what every American family deserves: to be together.”

Three overlooked court rulings limited White House anti-trans policies
Supreme Court narrowed trans rights, advocates saw victories in other decisions
Published
5 months ago on
July 7, 2026
By
Joe Reberkenny
(Bigstock photo)
While the U.S. Supreme Court&#8217;s decision in West Virginia v. B.P.J. continues to dominate headlines about transgender rights, three recent federal court cases produced significant rulings that limited or temporarily blocked Trump-Vance administration policies attacking trans Americans.
Talbott v. USA
Trump issued Executive Order 14183 , &#8220;Prioritizing Military Excellence and Readiness,&#8221; on Jan. 27, 2025, banning trans people from serving in the military. The following day, GLAD Law and the National Center for LGBTQ Rights filed a federal lawsuit in the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia challenging the ban on behalf of six active-duty service members and two individuals seeking to enlist. The organizations argue the policy violates the Fifth Amendment&#8217;s guarantee of equal protection under the law.
The plaintiffs sought a nationwide preliminary injunction — a temporary block on enforcement of the executive order while the litigation continued. The district court granted that injunction and later rejected the Trump-Vance administration&#8217;s request to dissolve it, temporarily protecting trans service members from being discharged solely because of their gender identity.
That protection, however, was short-lived. In Shilling v. Trump , the Supreme Court stayed the lower court&#8217;s injunction, allowing the military to begin enforcing the trans service ban while litigation continued. The U.S. Air Force subsequently required trans service members facing involuntary separation proceedings to appear in uniforms and grooming standards corresponding to their sex assigned at birth and, in some cases, used their deadnames during those proceedings.
Despite that setback, the plaintiffs secured two significant legal victories during Pride month.
On June 1, a federal appeals court blocked the discharge of the trans service members involved in Talbott. Then, on June 30, a federal district court certified the case as a class action on behalf of all currently serving trans service members . That means future rulings in the case will apply not only to the original six plaintiffs but to all active-duty trans military personnel covered by the class.
The case remains ongoing, but class certification significantly strengthens the ability to protect trans service members as the litigation continues. Currently, there are 28 plaintiffs in total, including the two still attempting to enlist.
Z.A. v. Blanche
In Z.A. v. Blanche (formerly Z.A. v. Lucile Salter Packard Children&#8217;s Hospital at Stanford), the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California issued an emergency order one day before a federal grand jury subpoena was set to be enforced on July 2. The order blocked the Department of Justice from obtaining confidential medical records belonging to California families whose children receive gender-affirming care.
The ruling relied in part on protections established under the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) , the 1996 federal law governing the privacy and security of medical records.
The decision represented a significant check on the administration&#8217;s efforts to obtain sensitive patient information, protecting the privacy of trans patients and their families while the legal challenge proceeds.
Doe v. Blanche
Doe v. Blanche , which remains ongoing, challenges Trump&#8217;s executive order, Defending Women From Gender Ideology Extremism and Restoring Biological Truth to the Federal Government . Under policies implementing that order, many trans women in federal custody would be housed in men&#8217;s prisons.
A federal district court in D.C. granted a preliminary injunction blocking enforcement of a Bureau of Prisons policy that would require incarcerated trans women to be housed in men&#8217;s facilities regardless of individualized safety assessments or the risk of sexual assault.
The Bureau of Prisons policy also conflicts with the goals of the Prison Rape Elimination Act (PREA), enacted by Congress in 2003 to address sexual abuse in correctional facilities through standards, research, funding, and prevention measures. Federal data has consistently shown that trans people in custody experience sexual assault at dramatically higher rates than the general prison population.
